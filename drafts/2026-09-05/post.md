## Rabbit White Korma with Cashew Cream

## Headnote

Rabbit white korma exists at the exact intersection where my two kitchens meet. Korma, in its Lahori register, is not the timid yellow sauce that arrived in British high streets. It is a slow, disciplined extraction of fat and spice, cooked until the oil separates and the meat has absorbed everything you've given it. Rabbit — *lapin* in the French butcher's language I've spoken for two decades — is the natural heir to that technique. Its lean, mild flesh needs both fat and aromatics to become interesting. Cashew paste provides the body that a classical Mughal korma would take from poppy seeds or blanched almonds, and heavy cream finishes it in a way that is unambiguously French, without apology. White pepper rather than black keeps the sauce pale and gives a clean, sustained heat. This is not a compromise dish. It is a dish that could only be made by someone who knows both traditions well enough to trust them.

## Ingredients

### Rabbit and marinade
- 1 whole rabbit, about 1.3 kg (2 lb 14 oz), jointed into 8 pieces (or 1.3 kg bone-in rabbit pieces)
- 150 g (⅔ cup) full-fat plain yoghurt
- 1 tsp fine sea salt
- 1 tsp ground white pepper
- ½ tsp ground ginger
- ½ tsp ground coriander (freshly ground, if possible)

### Cashew paste
- 120 g (scant 1 cup) raw cashews, soaked 30 min in hot water
- 80 ml (⅓ cup) warm water
- 1 tsp rose water (not essence; see ingredient notes)

### Korma base
- 60 ml (¼ cup) neutral oil (sunflower or rapeseed)
- 30 g (2 tbsp) unsalted butter
- 2 medium white onions, about 350 g (12 oz) total, thinly sliced
- 6 green cardamom pods, cracked
- 4 cloves
- 1 bay leaf
- 1 blade of mace (or ¼ tsp ground mace)
- 2.5 cm (1 in) piece of cassia bark (or cinnamon stick)
- 1 tsp ground white pepper
- ½ tsp ground ginger
- ½ tsp fine sea salt, plus more to taste
- 200 ml (scant 1 cup) warm water or unsalted chicken stock

### To finish
- 120 ml (½ cup) heavy cream (crème entière, 35% fat)
- 1 tsp kewra water (pandanus; see notes)
- Small pinch of saffron, steeped in 2 tbsp warm milk for 10 min
- Flaked almonds, lightly toasted, to serve
- A few fresh mint leaves, torn, to serve

## Ingredient notes

**Rabbit**: French supermarkets and halal butchers in larger European cities both carry whole lapin; ask your butcher to joint it if you prefer. If rabbit is unavailable, bone-in chicken thighs work but reduce braising time to 35 minutes. The dish changes character; it becomes a reliable white korma chicken, not the same thing.

**Rose water**: Buy it from a Pakistani or Middle Eastern grocer. The bottles sold in European pharmacies or baking aisles are often weaker or differently scented. One teaspoon of grocer's rose water equals roughly three teaspoons of the baking variety.

**Kewra water**: Distilled from the male pandanus flower. A Pakistani or Indian grocer stocks it. There is no direct substitute — omit it rather than use vanilla extract.

**Mace**: The aril of nutmeg, and the backbone of many Mughal white kormas. A single blade costs almost nothing and lasts a year in a sealed jar. Ground mace loses its top notes quickly, so use a blade if you have one.

**Cashews**: Raw, unsalted. Roasted cashews will turn the paste slightly bitter and darken the colour. Blanched almonds are a valid swap (same quantity, same method) and produce a slightly drier, nuttier sauce.

## Method

1. **Marinate the rabbit.** Mix the yoghurt, salt, white pepper, ground ginger and ground coriander in a large bowl. Add the rabbit pieces and turn to coat thoroughly. Cover and refrigerate for a minimum of 2 hours, or overnight. Bring to room temperature 30 minutes before cooking.

2. **Make the cashew paste.** Drain the soaked cashews, transfer to a blender with the 80 ml warm water, and blend on high for 2 minutes until completely smooth — no graininess at all. Add the rose water, pulse once to combine, and set aside. A high-powered blender produces a noticeably silkier result than a food processor.

3. **Fry the whole spices.** In a wide, heavy-bottomed pot (a 26 cm / 10 in casserole is ideal), heat the oil and butter together over medium heat until the butter foam subsides. Add the cardamom, cloves, bay leaf, mace and cassia. Stir for 60 to 90 seconds, until the cloves swell and the cassia uncurls slightly. This blooms the fat-soluble aromatics; the cashew sauce will carry their flavour throughout.

4. **Cook the onions deep.** Add the sliced onions and a pinch of salt. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring every few minutes, for 22 to 28 minutes, until the onions are deep mahogany, not amber. This is not optional — the sweetness of caramelised onion is what separates korma from a plain braised dish. If the onions colour unevenly, add a splash of water and keep cooking.

5. **Add ground spices and bhunno.** Add the ground white pepper and ground ginger. Stir constantly for 2 minutes. The oil may begin to separate at the edges of the pan — this is the sign that the spice rawness has been cooked out. Do not rush this step.

6. **Add the cashew paste and bhunno again.** Pour in the cashew paste. Cook over medium heat, stirring frequently, for 5 to 7 minutes. The paste will first bubble, then tighten, then begin to stick slightly and turn a faint golden at the edges. The oil will separate visibly again. This double bhunno is what gives the korma its depth.

7. **Brown the rabbit, briefly.** Push the masala to the sides of the pot. Increase the heat to medium-high. Add the marinated rabbit pieces (with any marinade clinging to them) and brown lightly on both sides, 2 to 3 minutes per side. You are not trying for a deep crust — just enough surface colour to add texture.

8. **Braise.** Add the warm water or stock, stir once to integrate the masala and the liquid, and bring to a gentle simmer. Cover tightly, reduce to low, and cook for 55 to 65 minutes, turning the rabbit pieces once at the halfway point. Rabbit is done when the meat near the hip joint pulls away from the bone without resistance. Saddle pieces will be done slightly earlier; check and remove them if needed.

9. **Finish the sauce.** Uncover the pot and raise the heat to medium. If there is excess liquid, reduce for 5 minutes. Add the cream, stir gently, and cook for 3 minutes until the sauce coats the back of a spoon. Add the kewra water and the saffron-steeped milk. Taste and adjust salt.

10. **Serve.** Transfer to a warmed serving dish. Scatter toasted flaked almonds and torn mint over the top. Serve with layered paratha, plain basmati, or — unorthodox but effective — a French baguette, which is how this dish is most often eaten at my table.

## Variations

**Bone-in chicken thighs**: Replace rabbit 1:1 by weight. Reduce braising time to 30 to 35 minutes. The dish will read as halal rabbit curry's more accessible cousin — still elegant, slightly richer in flavour because of higher fat content in thighs.

**Blanched almond paste instead of cashew**: Produces a slightly coarser, earthier sauce with a very faint bitterness that balances the cream beautifully. Soak 120 g blanched almonds for 1 hour in hot water before blending.

**Dairy-free**: Replace heavy cream with full-fat coconut cream (the thick kind, not coconut milk). The result has a faint tropical note that is not classical but not jarring either; increase the mace by half a blade to push back against it.

**Richer finish**: Stir in 1 tablespoon of good-quality ghee right at the end, off heat. This is the traditional Lahori move and it makes the surface glisten.

## Storage and reheating

Refrigerate in an airtight container for up to 3 days. The sauce thickens considerably when cold; reheat over low heat with 2 to 3 tablespoons of water stirred in, lid on, for 8 to 10 minutes, stirring gently once. Do not boil after the cream has been added — the sauce will split. Freeze the korma before adding cream (after step 8); defrost overnight in the refrigerator, then finish with cream on the stovetop.

## FAQ

### What does rabbit white korma taste like?
Rabbit white korma is a mild, deeply aromatic dish with a silky, cream-enriched sauce built from caramelised onions and cashew paste. The flavour is warm rather than hot, layered with cardamom, mace and white pepper, with no single spice dominating.

### Can I use chicken instead of rabbit in this white korma recipe?
Yes — bone-in chicken thighs are a direct swap at the same weight. Reduce the braising time to 30 to 35 minutes, as chicken cooks faster than rabbit and will become dry if over-braised.

### Is this a halal rabbit curry?
This rabbit white korma is fully halal: no alcohol, no pork products, and all ingredients are sourced halal. Rabbit is permissible in Hanafi fiqh, and the recipe uses no wine or spirits at any stage.

### Why does my korma sauce look grey instead of white?
Grey sauce is almost always caused by onions not cooked deeply enough (releasing water that dulls the colour) or by using roasted rather than raw cashews. Use raw cashews and blend them very smooth; the sauce should be a clean ivory.

### Can I make this rabbit white korma ahead of time?
Yes. Braise the rabbit and make the korma base up to 2 days ahead; refrigerate without the cream. On the day of serving, reheat gently, add the cream and aromatics, and finish for 5 minutes.

### Do I have to marinate the rabbit overnight?
Two hours is the minimum useful marination time for rabbit; overnight is better. The yoghurt acidulates and tenderises the lean muscle fibres, which rabbit has in abundance. Skipping the marinade entirely produces a noticeably firmer, drier result.

### Where do I buy rabbit in France or the UK?
In France, whole lapin is stocked by most supermarkets and all traditional butchers. In the UK, halal butchers in cities with a substantial Muslim population carry it; alternatively, specialist online butchers deliver whole rabbit nationwide. Ask for it jointed to save time.
