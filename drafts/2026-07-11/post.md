## Headnote

A tagine and a French braise are solving the same problem: take a tough, collagen-rich cut of meat and apply low, sustained heat until the connective tissue surrenders and the braising liquid becomes a sauce. The spice profile here is North African — ginger, turmeric, a pinch of saffron, preserved lemon — but the method is exactly what any French cook would recognise. Fat first, aromatics next, liquid to cover by half, lid on, leave it alone. What you get at the end is beef that pulls apart under light pressure and a sauce with enough body to coat the back of a spoon without any flour. The preserved lemon is not optional decoration; it does the acid work that wine would do in a French braise, brightening the whole dish. This is halal cooking that owes debts to two continents and is not ashamed of either.

## Ingredients

### Spice and aromatics
- 1 large onion, thinly sliced
- 3 garlic cloves, grated
- 1 tsp ground ginger
- 1 tsp ground turmeric
- ½ tsp ground black pepper
- 2 tsp salt, or to taste
- 1 pinch saffron threads (optional but recommended)
- 1 green cayenne chilli (optional)

### Main
- 500 g (1.1 lb) lean halal beef, cut into 4 cm (1.5 in) cubes
- 700 g (1.5 lb) waxy potatoes, peeled and cut into wedges
- 80 ml (⅓ cup) extra-virgin olive oil
- 700 ml (3 cups) water

### Finish
- 1 large handful green or red olives, unpitted or pitted
- ½ preserved lemon, cut into long quarters
- 1 small handful flat-leaf parsley or coriander, roughly chopped

## Ingredient notes

**Preserved lemon.** The rind is what you want; scrape out and discard the pulp before adding. Preserved lemon is cured in salt and its own juice, which ferments slightly over weeks. That fermentation produces a rounded, savoury acidity that fresh lemon cannot replicate. Find it in any North African or Middle Eastern grocery. If unavailable, use 1 tbsp fresh lemon juice plus ½ tsp finely grated zest — it will not be the same, but the dish remains coherent.

**Saffron.** A pinch means roughly 20 threads. Crumble them between your fingers directly into the water before adding. Do not steep them in boiling water separately; at this scale and cook time, direct addition is fine.

**Halal beef.** Shoulder or chuck is better here than the lean stewing beef the original calls for. Lean cuts work, but collagen-rich cuts produce a naturally thickened sauce. If you use lean beef, accept that you may need to reduce the liquid uncovered at the end.

**Olives.** Moroccan oil-cured olives, cracked green olives, or a mix — all work. Rinse them if they are very salty. Unpitted olives hold their shape better during cooking; warn your table.

**Waxy potatoes.** Charlotte, Nicola, or similar. Floury potatoes will disintegrate into the sauce, which is not the goal. You want distinct wedges that have absorbed the braising liquid without collapsing.

## Method

1. **Combine the spice base with the beef.** In a wide, heavy-bottomed pot or a tagine base, combine the beef cubes, sliced onion, grated garlic, ginger, turmeric, black pepper, salt, chilli if using, and olive oil. Mix with your hands or a large spoon until the beef is thoroughly coated. This dry spice coating gives the beef surface flavour before any liquid is added.

2. **Start over medium heat.** Place the pot over medium heat and cook, stirring occasionally, for about eight minutes. You are not searing for a crust here; you are sweating the onion and toasting the spices gently in the oil until the raw smell of turmeric softens and the onion begins to turn translucent and slightly golden at the edges.

3. **Add water and saffron.** Pour in the 700 ml of water and crumble in the saffron. Stir once to distribute. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce the heat to a steady simmer. The liquid should move but not thrash. Cover with a lid slightly ajar to allow some steam to escape.

4. **Braise the beef for 20 minutes.** Leave the beef to braise for 20 minutes undisturbed. At this point the beef is not yet tender, but it is halfway. The saffron will have turned the broth a pale gold.

5. **Add the potatoes.** Arrange the potato wedges over and around the beef. Do not stir them in aggressively; you want them to cook in the liquid while holding their shape. Replace the lid, still ajar, and continue simmering for 20 more minutes.

6. **Check the potatoes and sauce.** At 20 minutes, test a potato wedge with a paring knife. It should offer almost no resistance. The sauce at this point should have reduced to something between a broth and a gravy. If it is still very thin, remove the lid entirely and increase the heat to medium for five minutes to reduce.

7. **Add the preserved lemon and olives.** Lay the preserved lemon quarters and the olives over the top. Do not bury them; they only need three to four minutes of heat to warm through and release their brine into the sauce. Replace the lid for this final stage.

8. **Finish and serve.** Scatter the parsley or coriander over the top. Serve directly from the pot. Couscous or bread for the sauce; nothing else is needed.

## Variations

**Lamb shoulder instead of beef.** Cut bone-in or boneless lamb shoulder into 4 cm cubes. The technique is identical; the braise time reduces slightly, around 35 minutes total rather than 40, because lamb shoulder has more fat and tenderises faster.

**Chicken thighs.** Bone-in, skin-on halal chicken thighs replace the beef with no spice changes. Reduce total braise time to 30 minutes. Skip the potato or add it at the very start so it has time to cook. The sauce will be lighter.

**No potatoes, add courgette.** Courgette cut into thick rounds (2 cm) added in the final 15 minutes replaces the potatoes entirely for a lighter summer version. Do not add them earlier or they dissolve.

**More heat.** Two green cayenne chillies rather than one, sliced rather than left whole, changes the dish noticeably. The saffron still comes through, but there is a clear burn on the finish.

## Storage and reheating

Allow to cool to room temperature within two hours of cooking. Refrigerate in a sealed container for up to three days. The sauce thickens considerably when cold, which is normal — the potato starch has leached into it.

Reheat over low heat with two to three tablespoons of water added to loosen the sauce. Stir gently; the potatoes are fragile after the first cook. Do not microwave without covering, or the potatoes dry out. Freezing is possible but the potatoes turn slightly grainy on thawing — still edible, not ideal. If you plan to freeze, add the potatoes fresh when reheating.

## FAQ

### Can I use a slow cooker?
Yes. Complete step one and two on the hob first to soften the onion and bloom the spices. Transfer everything to the slow cooker, add water and saffron, and cook on low for six hours or high for three and a half. Add the preserved lemon and olives in the final 30 minutes. The sauce will not reduce naturally, so remove the lid and switch to high for the last 20 minutes if you want it thicker.

### My sauce is too thin. What went wrong?
The beef released more water than expected, or the lid was too tight and no steam escaped. Fix it simply: remove the meat and potatoes to a warm plate, increase the heat to medium-high, and reduce the sauce uncovered until it coats a spoon. Two to three minutes is usually enough. Return the meat and potatoes, add the preserved lemon and olives, and proceed.

### The preserved lemon is very salty. Should I rinse it?
A quick rinse under cold water is reasonable if your preserved lemon is aggressively salty. Taste the rind before adding. The salt it contributes should season the sauce, not overpower it. Adjust the added salt in the recipe accordingly.

### Can I make this in a real clay tagine?
A clay tagine on a heat diffuser over low gas produces the best result for flavour, because the steam cycles inside the conical lid and bastes the meat continuously. The total time increases to around 90 minutes because you must keep the heat lower. Do not place a cold clay tagine over high direct heat; it will crack.

### Do the olives need to be pitted?
No, and leaving them unpitted means they hold together better during cooking. Just make this clear when you serve, especially if children are eating.

### What is the right beef cut for this?
Shoulder or chuck is better than lean stewing beef because the collagen in those cuts dissolves into the sauce and gives it body without any thickening agent. Lean beef works but produces a thinner sauce. If your butcher labels it 'beef for stew', ask specifically for shoulder.

### Can I add other vegetables?
Carrots cut into thick diagonal rounds go in with the potatoes and work well. Fennel quartered lengthwise adds an anise note that plays well with the preserved lemon. Green beans added in the final ten minutes stay bright and offer a textural contrast. Avoid soft vegetables like tomatoes unless you want them to dissolve into the sauce background.
