## Butternut Squash Cumin Soup with Ghee Tarka

## Headnote

This butternut squash cumin soup started as a French velouté — roasted squash, sweated aromatics, cream, blended smooth. That version was good. Then one evening I finished a bowl with a small spoonful of ghee tarka: whole cumin seeds dropped into hot ghee until they crackled and darkened, then poured over the surface. It changed the dish completely. The fat carried the cumin's volatile oils across the entire surface of the soup; every spoonful tasted smoky and warm rather than just sweet and creamy. This is the technique the French call *monter au beurre* repurposed for a Pakistani reflex. Neither tradition would claim this soup, but both would recognize what is happening inside it.

## Ingredients

### Soup base
- 1 kg (2.2 lb) butternut squash, peeled and cut into 3 cm (1¼ in) cubes
- 2 tbsp neutral oil (sunflower or rapeseed)
- 1 medium onion (about 180 g / 6 oz), roughly chopped
- 3 garlic cloves, smashed
- 1 tsp ground coriander
- ½ tsp ground turmeric
- ½ tsp white pepper
- 900 ml (3¾ cups) good chicken or vegetable stock
- 80 ml (⅓ cup) double cream
- Salt to taste

### Ghee cumin tarka
- 2 tbsp ghee
- 1½ tsp whole cumin seeds
- ½ tsp Kashmiri chilli powder (or mild chilli flakes)
- Pinch of hing

### To serve
- Small handful of fresh coriander leaves
- A few drops of lemon juice per bowl

## Ingredient Notes

**Butternut squash:** The variety matters less than the moisture content. Look for a squash that feels heavy for its size and has a matte, not shiny, skin. Shiny skin often means it was picked early and will be watery and bland. Roasting rather than boiling is non-negotiable here — it drives off moisture and concentrates the sugars.

**Ghee:** Use proper clarified ghee, not vegetable ghee. The milk solids have been removed, which means it can reach a higher temperature before burning — exactly what you need for a good tarka. Supermarket ghee works fine.

**Whole cumin seeds:** The tarka uses whole seeds, not ground. Ground cumin would dissolve into the ghee and disappear; whole seeds blister and pop, releasing the essential oils in a controlled burst. You want that textural punctuation on the surface of the soup.

**Hing:** A tiny pinch — less than ⅛ tsp — goes into the tarka. It rounds the cumin without asserting itself. Skip it if you don't have it; do not substitute.

**Kashmiri chilli powder:** This is for colour and mild warmth, not heat. The soup should be gently warm, not hot. If you use a hotter variety, halve the quantity.

**Stock:** A good stock changes this soup more than anything else. If using carton stock, reduce it by a quarter first over medium heat before adding it to the pot.

## Method

1. **Roast the squash.** Preheat the oven to 200°C / 400°F. Toss the cubed squash with 1 tbsp of the oil and a generous pinch of salt. Spread on a baking tray in a single layer — crowding steams rather than roasts. Roast for 25 to 30 minutes until the edges are caramelized and the flesh is completely soft. The caramelization matters: it is where the depth comes from.

2. **Sweat the aromatics.** While the squash roasts, warm the remaining 1 tbsp oil in a large saucepan over medium-low heat. Add the onion with a pinch of salt and cook, stirring occasionally, for 12 to 15 minutes until soft and a pale gold — not mahogany, not raw-white. Add the garlic and cook for another 2 minutes.

3. **Add the dry spices.** Stir in the ground coriander, turmeric, and white pepper. Cook for 60 seconds, stirring constantly, until fragrant. The spices need the brief dry heat to open up; they will taste dusty if you skip this step.

4. **Combine and simmer.** Add the roasted squash and the stock to the pot. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for 10 minutes. This short simmer integrates the roasted squash flavour into the liquid rather than just blending two separate things.

5. **Blend until completely smooth.** Use a stick blender directly in the pot, or transfer in batches to a standing blender. Blend for at least 90 seconds — this is a velouté, not a rustic puree. The texture should be completely smooth and coat the back of a spoon with some resistance.

6. **Add the cream and adjust seasoning.** Return the pot to low heat. Stir in the double cream and heat gently — do not boil after the cream goes in or the texture can split. Taste and correct the salt. If the soup tastes flat, a few drops of lemon juice (not cream) will fix it.

7. **Make the tarka.** Heat the ghee in a small pan over medium-high heat. When the ghee shimmers and a single cumin seed dropped in sizzles immediately, add all the cumin seeds. They will crackle and darken in 20 to 30 seconds. Watch them — the difference between fragrant and burnt is about 10 seconds. Remove from heat, add the Kashmiri chilli powder and hing, and swirl once. The residual heat does the rest.

8. **Serve immediately.** Ladle the soup into warm bowls. Pour the tarka, ghee and all, over the surface of each bowl. Finish with a few coriander leaves and a drop or two of lemon juice. Serve at once — the tarka aroma fades quickly.

## Variations

**Fully vegetarian / vegan:** Replace the chicken stock with a deep vegetable stock (one made with roasted vegetables, not a carton of watery liquid). Replace the double cream with full-fat coconut cream — it adds a faint sweetness that works with the squash. Replace the ghee in the tarka with refined coconut oil.

**Roasted cumin squash soup with ginger:** Add a 3 cm piece of fresh ginger, peeled and grated, along with the garlic in step 2. It sharpens the soup without adding chilli heat — good if you are serving it to children who tolerate ginger.

**Richer finish, without cream:** Swirl 30 g (2 tbsp) of cold, unsalted butter into the blended soup off the heat rather than using cream. This is the classical French *monter au beurre* finish — it adds richness and a slight gloss without the dairy sweetness.

**Spiced butternut soup with whole spices:** Add a 5 cm (2 in) cinnamon stick and two green cardamom pods, lightly crushed, to the oil before the onions in step 2. Remove them before blending. The soup becomes warmer and more aromatic — better suited to a formal dinner than a weeknight bowl.

## Storage and Reheating

Store the soup and the tarka separately. The soup keeps in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to 4 days or in the freezer for up to 3 months. Do not freeze the cream-enriched version — freeze before adding the cream, then add it fresh when reheating.

Reheat the soup gently over low heat, stirring, until just below a simmer. Make a fresh tarka each time — it takes 3 minutes and the difference between a reheated tarka and a fresh one is substantial.

## FAQ

### Can I make butternut squash cumin soup without roasting the squash first?
You can skip roasting and simply simmer raw cubed squash in the stock, but the soup will taste noticeably sweeter and thinner. Roasting drives off water and caramelizes the squash's natural sugars, which gives this butternut squash cumin soup its depth.

### What is a tarka and why does it go on soup?
A tarka (also called chaunk or tadka) is whole spices bloomed in hot fat, then poured over food as a finishing step. The hot fat carries fat-soluble aromatic compounds from the spices across the surface of the dish, distributing flavour far more efficiently than stirring ground spices into the base would.

### Can I use ground cumin instead of whole seeds in the tarka?
No — ground cumin burns almost instantly in hot ghee and turns bitter before it can do its work. Whole seeds release their oils more slowly and develop a toasty, slightly smoky character that is the point of this finishing step.

### How do I know when the cumin seeds are done in the tarka without burning them?
The seeds are ready when they have darkened one full shade (from beige to medium brown) and smell toasty rather than raw. Remove the pan from heat the moment you smell them — the residual heat continues cooking them for another 5 to 10 seconds.

### Can I substitute the double cream with something lighter?
Full-fat crème fraîche works well and adds a slight tang. Single cream makes the soup thinner. Coconut cream is a good dairy-free option. Do not use low-fat versions of anything — the fat is structural here, not optional richness.

### Is this soup freezer-friendly?
Yes, but freeze it before adding the cream. Cream-enriched soups can separate on thawing. Cool the blended, unseasoned soup completely, portion into 400 ml (1⅔ cup) containers, and freeze for up to 3 months. Add fresh cream when reheating.

### What stock works best in this veloute de courge?
A light, well-made chicken stock gives the cleanest result. A roasted vegetable stock is the best vegetarian option. Avoid stocks with strong flavours (like dark beef stock) — they compete with the squash rather than supporting it.
