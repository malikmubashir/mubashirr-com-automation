## Headnote

The French do one thing with roast chicken that most people never bother with: they dry the bird completely, rub butter or fat into the skin rather than under it, and trust the oven to do the rest. No basting every fifteen minutes. No foil tent. The result is skin that actually cracks when you press it. I wanted that technique, and I wanted it to taste like the tikka I grew up eating in Lahore — the kind made in a proper tandoor, where the yogurt and spices char at the edges without drying the meat. Rose harissa is the bridge. It adds the heat and the smokiness that the oven can simulate but not quite replicate, and its floral note changes the overall character of the dish from straightforward tikka to something genuinely its own. This is not tikka. It is not poulet rôti. It is what happens when you think about both at the same time.

## Ingredients

### The bird
- 1 whole chicken, 1.5–1.7 kg (3.3–3.7 lb), halal
- 1.5 tsp fine sea salt (for initial dry salting)

### Tikka marinade
- 120 g (½ cup) full-fat plain yogurt
- 2 tbsp rose harissa (see ingredient notes)
- 4 garlic cloves, grated to a paste
- 2 tsp fresh ginger, grated
- 1.5 tsp Kashmiri red chilli powder
- 1 tsp cumin powder
- 1 tsp coriander powder
- ½ tsp garam masala
- ½ tsp smoked paprika
- ½ tsp turmeric
- ¼ tsp hing
- 1.5 tsp fine sea salt
- 2 tbsp neutral oil (sunflower or rapeseed)
- Juice of half a lemon

### For roasting
- 1 medium onion, halved
- 1 lemon, halved
- 4 garlic cloves, unpeeled
- 2 tbsp unsalted butter, softened
- Flaky salt, to finish

## Ingredient notes

**Rose harissa** — This is harissa made with dried rose petals, and it is a different product from plain harissa. The rose adds a faint floral bitterness that cuts through the richness of the yogurt and the fat. Belazu and Mina both make widely available halal versions; check the label. If you can only find standard harissa, use it with a few drops of rose water stirred in — it approximates the effect without exactly replicating it.

**Hing** — Asafoetida resin powder. In tikka marinades it works the way shallots work in French cooking: it adds depth and allium complexity without being identifiable. Use a tiny pinch. Too much smells like a gas leak.

**Kashmiri red chilli powder** — Essential here. It delivers the brick-red colour and a gentle, building warmth rather than sharp heat. Regular red chilli powder is hotter and less complex; reduce the quantity by a third if substituting.

**Yogurt** — Full-fat only. Low-fat yogurt has too much water, which steams the chicken instead of charring the spices. Greek yogurt works and produces a thicker coating that holds better during roasting.

## Method

1. **Salt the bird (8–24 hours before cooking).** Pat the chicken thoroughly dry with paper towels — inside the cavity as well. Sprinkle 1.5 tsp fine salt over the outside and inside. Place on a wire rack set over a tray, uncovered, in the refrigerator. This draws surface moisture out and then reabsorbs it as seasoning. It also dries the skin, which is the first step toward crispness.

2. **Make the marinade.** Combine all marinade ingredients in a bowl and whisk until smooth. The mixture should be the colour of deep terracotta. Taste it: it should be salty, warm, and slightly floral. Adjust.

3. **Marinate the chicken.** Remove the chicken from the rack. Rub the marinade firmly over every surface — under the neck flap, into the cavity, between the leg and breast where you can work your fingers without tearing the skin. Do not push marinade under the breast skin; that skin will not crisp properly if it is wet underneath. Return the chicken to the rack, uncovered, in the refrigerator for at least 8 hours and up to 24. The yogurt enzymes and the acid begin breaking down the outer protein fibres. This is why tikka is tender.

4. **Bring to room temperature.** Remove the chicken from the refrigerator 45 minutes before roasting. A cold bird takes longer to cook through and the skin starts to scorch before the interior is done.

5. **Preheat the oven to 220°C (425°F), conventional heat, no fan.** Place the onion halves, lemon halves, and unpeeled garlic cloves in the centre of a roasting tin to form a rough trivet. This lifts the bird slightly and prevents the bottom from steaming.

6. **Butter the top.** Using your fingers, smear the softened butter across the breast and legs. The butter accelerates browning on the skin and carries fat-soluble flavour compounds from the spices outward. Set the chicken breast-side up on the trivet.

7. **Roast.** Place the tin in the centre of the oven. Do not baste. Do not open the oven for the first 45 minutes. After 45 minutes, check the skin: it should be deeply charred at the spice edges, with the yogurt coating darkened. Reduce the temperature to 190°C (375°F) and roast for a further 20–25 minutes.

8. **Check doneness.** Insert a probe thermometer into the thickest part of the thigh, away from bone. The target is 74°C (165°F). Alternatively, pierce the thigh joint: the juices should run clear with no pink. If the skin is charring too quickly before the interior is done, lay a sheet of foil loosely over the breast only.

9. **Rest.** Transfer the chicken to a warm plate and rest for 15 minutes before carving. Resting allows the muscle fibres to reabsorb the cooking juices. Cutting too early wastes them on the board.

10. **Finish and serve.** Press the roasted garlic cloves out of their skins and squeeze the caramelised lemon halves over the resting juices in the tin. Stir this together and spoon over the carved chicken. Finish with flaky salt.

## Variations

**Cheaper cut: bone-in chicken thighs.** Use eight bone-in, skin-on thighs. Reduce roasting time to 35 minutes at 210°C (410°F). The marinade and technique are identical. Thighs are more forgiving with the high heat.

**Vegetarian version: whole cauliflower.** A 1 kg head of cauliflower, core trimmed so it sits flat, roasted with the same marinade at 200°C (390°F) for 45–50 minutes. The cauliflower chars well and absorbs the marinade deeply. Not the same dish; a good one.

**Milder version for children.** Replace the harissa with 1 tbsp tomato paste and reduce Kashmiri chilli powder to ½ tsp. Add ½ tsp of dried fenugreek leaves (kasuri methi). The result is gentler, still aromatic, and less confrontationally red.

**Additional smoke.** If you have a grill setting on your oven, switch to it for the final five minutes of cooking with the oven door slightly ajar. This replicates, approximately, what a tandoor does at the end. The skin blisters.

## Storage and reheating

Store carved leftovers in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to three days. Reheat in a 180°C (355°F) oven on a wire rack for 12–15 minutes; this preserves more of the skin texture than a microwave. The microwave is acceptable for speed but the skin will soften. Leftover chicken, shredded cold, works well in a wrap with raita and shredded cabbage.

Do not freeze cooked chicken with this marinade; the yogurt coating becomes grainy and the texture of the meat suffers.

## FAQ

### Can I skip the overnight marinade?
You can marinate for as little as four hours and the flavour will still be there, but the texture of the meat will be noticeably less tender. The yogurt needs time to work. If you are short on time, score the thighs and drumsticks deeply with a knife before applying the marinade; this shortens the effective penetration time.

### Is this harissa tikka roast chicken actually halal?
Yes, provided you use a halal-certified whole chicken and a halal-certified harissa. Both Belazu and Mina rose harissas are halal; verify your local stock with the label. There is no alcohol or pork in this recipe.

### My yogurt marinade is sliding off the chicken. What am I doing wrong?
The chicken was not dry enough before marinating. Pat it again with paper towels and return it to the rack for another 30 minutes uncovered before applying the marinade. Also, thick Greek yogurt holds better than thin plain yogurt. A marinade that slides means a coating that falls off in the oven.

### The skin is burning before the chicken is cooked through. How do I fix this?
Your oven runs hot, or the chicken is larger than 1.7 kg. Cover the breast loosely with foil after the first 40 minutes and reduce the temperature by 10°C. The goal is deeply charred, not carbonised. The spices will always darken more than a plain bird; this is correct and expected.

### What do I serve with this?
A simple cucumber and mint raita on one side, and either roasted flatbread or pommes de terre sarladaises (duck-fat potatoes with garlic and parsley) on the other. The French and Pakistani elements hold together at the table the same way they do in the recipe.

### Can I use boneless chicken breasts instead?
Boneless breasts dry out badly at the temperatures needed to char the spices. If you want boneless, use thighs. They have the fat content to survive the heat and stay moist.

### Do I need a meat thermometer?
It makes the difference between a well-cooked bird and an overcooked one. A probe thermometer costs less than ten euros and eliminates guesswork. Use it.
