## Headnote

Duck breast karahi exists because two things are true simultaneously: magret de canard is one of the finest everyday cuts in a French butcher's cabinet, and karahi is one of the fastest, most honest cooking methods I know. The duck's dense, iron-rich meat handles high heat and strong spice without vanishing into the background. The fat renders into the tomato base and replaces any need for cooking oil beyond the initial sear. There is nothing experimental about this on either side. A Lahori karahi is already a high-heat, wet-dry, tightly spiced technique. A magret is already accustomed to a hot pan and a short rest. Bringing them together is less fusion than it is recognition.

## Ingredients

### Duck
- 2 halal duck breasts (magret de canard), approximately 300 g (10.5 oz) each
- 1 tsp fine sea salt
- ½ tsp coarsely cracked black pepper

### Karahi Masala
- 4 medium tomatoes, roughly chopped (about 400 g / 14 oz)
- 30 g (1 oz) fresh ginger, peeled and cut into thin matchsticks (half reserved for finishing)
- 4 green chillies, slit lengthwise (seeds in for heat, out for medium)
- 1 tsp cumin seeds
- 1 tsp coriander powder
- ½ tsp Kashmiri red chilli powder
- ½ tsp garam masala
- ¼ tsp turmeric
- 1 tsp fine sea salt, plus more to taste

### Finishing
- Small bunch fresh coriander (cilantro), roughly chopped
- Reserved ginger matchsticks
- 1 green chilli, sliced thin on the diagonal
- 1 tsp lemon juice

## Ingredient Notes

**Halal duck breast (magret de canard):** In France, halal magret de canard is sold at larger halal butchers and increasingly at Carrefour and Auchan halal sections. Outside France, any halal duck breast works. A magret specifically comes from a duck fattened for foie gras, which means more subcutaneous fat — this matters because that fat is the cooking medium. A standard duck breast from a non-fattened duck will have less fat under the skin; reduce your initial render time by two minutes.

**Kashmiri red chilli powder:** Mild, deeply coloured, earthy. Not a substitute for cayenne. If unavailable, use ¼ tsp sweet paprika and ¼ tsp standard red chilli powder combined.

**Ginger matchsticks:** Slice the ginger across the grain into 2 mm coins, then stack and cut into matchsticks. Half goes in early for depth; half goes in raw at the end for brightness. These are two different ingredients despite being the same thing.

## Method

1. **Score the duck skin.** Use a sharp knife to cut a crosshatch pattern through the skin and fat of each breast, going down to but not into the flesh. Cuts should be about 1 cm (½ in) apart. Season the skin side with half the salt and all the black pepper.

2. **Render the fat.** Place the duck breasts skin-side down in a cold wok or karahi. Turn the heat to medium. Allow the fat to render slowly for 8–10 minutes until the skin is deep golden and the fat has visibly pooled around the breasts. Do not rush this. The rendered fat is your cooking oil for the entire dish.

3. **Sear the flesh side.** Increase heat to medium-high. Flip the breasts and sear the flesh side for 90 seconds only. The meat should be deeply browned but still rare inside — it finishes in the gravy. Remove the duck to a board. Leave all the rendered fat in the pan.

4. **Bloom the spices.** Add the cumin seeds to the hot duck fat. Within 30 seconds they should be sizzling and fragrant. Add the coriander powder, Kashmiri chilli, and turmeric. Stir for 20 seconds. The fat will turn orange-red.

5. **Add tomatoes and ginger.** Tip in the chopped tomatoes, half the ginger matchsticks, and the slit green chillies. Add salt. Cook on high heat, stirring frequently, for 10–12 minutes. The tomatoes must collapse entirely and the masala must begin to separate — meaning you see the red fat pooling at the edges of a thickened paste, not a wet sauce. If tomatoes release too much water, increase heat and reduce aggressively.

6. **Slice the duck.** While the masala reduces, slice each breast at a 45-degree angle into 8 mm (⅓ in) pieces. The centre should still be visibly pink.

7. **Finish the duck in the gravy.** Reduce heat to medium. Add the duck slices to the thickened masala. Toss to coat. Cook for 2–3 minutes, turning once. The duck finishes to medium-rare to medium inside the spiced gravy. Any longer and the breast turns dry.

8. **Add garam masala and finish.** Scatter over the garam masala, lemon juice, fresh coriander, reserved raw ginger matchsticks, and the sliced green chilli. Toss once. Taste for salt. Serve immediately.

## Variations

**Leaner duck:** If using non-magret duck breasts with less subcutaneous fat, add 1 tbsp neutral oil (sunflower or rapeseed) before blooming the spices in step 4 to compensate for the reduced rendered fat.

**Add capsicum:** Lahori karahi often includes strips of green capsicum added with the tomatoes. 100 g (3.5 oz) of thin-sliced green pepper added in step 5 adds texture and tempers the heat slightly.

**Chicken karahi base:** The masala recipe here is the same architecture as a chicken karahi. If halal duck is genuinely unavailable, bone-in chicken thighs work; increase the cook time in step 7 to 12–15 minutes until the meat is cooked through.

**Black pepper forward version:** Traditional Peshawar-style karahi uses almost no red chilli, relying on cracked black pepper and ginger for heat. Omit Kashmiri chilli powder, double the black pepper in the marinade, and increase ginger to 40 g (1.5 oz) total. The result is paler and sharper-tasting, and it pairs interestingly with the duck's mineral notes.

## Storage and Reheating

Transfer leftovers to an airtight container and refrigerate within one hour of cooking. Keeps for up to two days. The duck will continue to cook slightly in residual heat as it cools; any pink at the centre will be gone by the time you reheat.

To reheat: place in a small saucepan or karahi with 2 tbsp of water over medium heat, covered, for 4–5 minutes. Stir once. Do not microwave on high — it toughens the duck. Add a fresh squeeze of lemon and a few coriander leaves before serving again.

This does not freeze well. Duck breast, once cooked and sliced, loses texture on freezing.

## FAQ

### What makes duck breast karahi different from chicken karahi?
Duck breast karahi uses the rendered fat from the duck skin itself as the cooking medium, which gives the masala a richer, meatier base than the neutral oil used in chicken karahi. The duck also stays sliced rather than cooked on the bone, so the method is faster.

### Can I use a regular duck breast instead of magret de canard for this karahi?
Yes. A standard halal duck breast works in this duck breast karahi recipe; it will have less subcutaneous fat, so compensate by adding one tablespoon of neutral oil when blooming the spices.

### How do I know when the karahi masala is ready before adding the duck?
The masala is ready when the tomatoes have fully disintegrated and bright orange-red oil is visibly separating around the edges of the paste. A wet, sauce-like consistency means more time is needed.

### Is magret de canard the same as ordinary duck breast?
Magret de canard specifically refers to the breast of a duck raised for foie gras production, which gives it a thicker fat cap and more flavour. Ordinary duck breast and magret can be used interchangeably in cooking, with minor adjustments to fat and cooking time.

### How do I find halal duck breast in France?
Halal magret de canard is available at halal butchers in most major French cities and at the halal sections of large supermarkets like Carrefour, Auchan, and Leclerc. Online halal meat retailers in France also stock it, often frozen.

### Can I cook the duck all the way through instead of medium?
You can, but duck breast overcooked loses most of its texture and becomes dry and grainy inside the masala. Cooking to medium (a slightly pink centre) is the intended result. If you prefer well-done, reduce the slicing thickness to 5 mm and increase step 7 to four minutes.

### What do I serve with duck breast karahi?
Flatbread (naan or roti) is the traditional pairing for karahi and works well here. Plain steamed basmati is also good. Avoid heavily spiced accompaniments — the duck's richness benefits from something neutral alongside it.
