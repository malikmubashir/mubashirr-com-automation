## Beef Bourguignon Pasanda

The French learned long ago that a tough cut of beef, given time and liquid and aromatics, will repay you generously. The Pakistanis learned something similar, threading whole spices through yogurt-marinated meat and letting heat do its slow work. This recipe does not pretend the two are the same tradition; they are not. What they share is patience and the understanding that the right fat, the right heat, and the right acid turn something cheap into something worth sitting down for.

The papaya in the marinade is doing real enzymatic work on the connective tissue — this is not garnish. The French shallots, glazed in their own juice until jammy, replace the pearl onions of a classic bourguignon and echo the sweetness a good Bourgogne would have provided. This is not coq au vin with beef; it is what bourguignon teaches you to make when you cook without wine.

## Ingredients

### Marinade
- 1 kg (2.2 lb) beef shin or chuck, cut into 5 cm (2 in) pieces
- 80 g (3 oz) raw papaya, grated (about 3 tablespoons pulp)
- 150 g (5 oz) full-fat plain yogurt
- 1 teaspoon fine salt
- 1 teaspoon ground white pepper
- 1 teaspoon ground cumin
- 1 teaspoon ground coriander

### Whole Spice Base
- 4 green cardamom pods, lightly cracked
- 2 black cardamom pods, lightly cracked
- 1 cinnamon stick, 5 cm (2 in)
- 4 cloves
- 1 dried bay leaf
- 8 black peppercorns
- 1 star anise

### Braise
- 50 ml (3½ tablespoons) neutral oil (or clarified butter)
- 300 g (10.5 oz) shallots, peeled and left whole if small, halved if large
- 4 garlic cloves, thinly sliced
- 2 cm (¾ in) piece fresh ginger, minced
- 2 medium tomatoes, roughly chopped (about 200 g / 7 oz)
- 1 tablespoon tomato paste
- 600 ml (2½ cups) good beef stock
- 150 ml (⅔ cup) verjus (unfermented grape juice; see ingredient notes)
- 1 teaspoon kashmiri red chilli powder
- Salt to taste

### To finish
- 2 tablespoons unsalted butter
- Small handful flat-leaf parsley, roughly chopped
- 1 tablespoon fresh lemon juice

## Ingredient Notes

**Raw papaya:** Green (unripe) papaya contains papain, a protease enzyme that breaks down muscle fibres. You need unripe papaya — the flesh should be white and firm, not orange. Grate it on the fine side of a box grater and use it within two hours of grating; the enzyme loses activity quickly. South Asian grocery stores carry it year-round. Kiwi fruit pulp is a reasonable substitute but acts faster, so limit marinating time to two hours maximum.

**Verjus:** Pressed juice of unripe grapes, sold in specialty food shops and online. It provides the acidity and faint fruity depth that wine would give a bourguignon, without alcohol. It is not identical — the tannin structure is absent — but it is not a poor cousin either. Pomegranate juice (200 ml, reduce stock by 50 ml to compensate) is a good Pakistani-leaning alternative with a more assertive sour note.

**Beef stock:** Homemade or a good commercial block, not powder. The braise reduces by roughly a third; weak stock produces a thin, one-note sauce.

**Beef shin vs. chuck:** Shin has more collagen and produces a silkier sauce. Chuck is slightly quicker to become tender. Both work; shin is the better choice if you can find it.

**Kashmiri red chilli powder:** Mild heat, deep brick colour. Deggi mirch is interchangeable. Regular chilli powder will sharpen the heat considerably — halve the quantity if substituting.

## Method

1. **Marinate the beef.** Combine the grated papaya pulp, yogurt, salt, white pepper, cumin, and coriander in a large bowl. Add the beef pieces and turn well to coat every surface. Cover and refrigerate for a minimum of 4 hours, maximum 6 hours. Beyond 6 hours the papain begins to make the surface of the meat mealy rather than tender.

2. **Bring the beef to room temperature.** Remove the bowl from the refrigerator 45 minutes before you intend to cook. Cold meat dropped into a hot pan cools the surface fat immediately and you get steaming rather than browning.

3. **Brown the shallots.** Heat the oil in a wide, heavy-based pot (a 26–28 cm / 10–11 in enamelled cast iron is ideal) over medium heat. Add the shallots cut-side down and cook without stirring for 6–8 minutes until deep golden on the cut face. Remove and set aside. They will finish cooking in the braise.

4. **Bloom the whole spices.** In the same pot over medium-low heat, add the cardamom, black cardamom, cinnamon, cloves, bay leaf, peppercorns, and star anise. Stir for 60–90 seconds until you smell the oils opening up. Do not let them scorch; they bitter quickly.

5. **Add garlic and ginger.** Add the sliced garlic and minced ginger directly to the spices. Stir for 2 minutes until pale gold.

6. **Brown the beef in batches.** Scrape as much marinade off the meat as you can — excess yogurt will steam rather than sear. Working in two batches, brown the beef pieces over medium-high heat, about 3 minutes per side until deep mahogany, not amber. Transfer each batch to a plate. The fond on the bottom of the pan is where flavour lives; do not discard it.

7. **Build the tomato base.** Add the chopped tomatoes and tomato paste to the pot. Cook over medium heat, stirring and pressing the tomatoes, for 8–10 minutes until the mixture is thick, dark red, and beginning to separate from the oil. This step concentrates the tomato sugars and drives off raw acidity.

8. **Add the chilli powder.** Stir in the kashmiri chilli powder and cook for 1 minute. It blooms in the hot fat and gives the sauce its deep brick colour.

9. **Deglaze with verjus.** Pour in the verjus and scrape the bottom of the pot firmly with a wooden spoon to lift the fond. Let it reduce for 3 minutes.

10. **Return the beef and add stock.** Nestle the browned beef back into the pot. Pour in the stock. The liquid should come roughly two-thirds up the meat — not submerge it. Bring to a gentle simmer.

11. **Add the shallots and braise.** Return the browned shallots to the pot. Cover tightly, reduce heat to the lowest possible simmer, and cook for 2 hours. Check every 45 minutes; the liquid should be barely bubbling. If your stove runs hot, use a heat diffuser or transfer to an oven at 150°C (300°F) after step 10.

12. **Check for tenderness.** After 2 hours, prod the beef with a fork. It should yield without resistance and begin to flake at the edges. If not, cover and cook for a further 30–45 minutes.

13. **Reduce and finish the sauce.** Remove the beef and shallots to a warm plate. Increase heat to medium and reduce the braising liquid for 10–12 minutes until it coats a spoon lightly. Taste and adjust salt. Off the heat, stir in the butter and lemon juice — the butter gives the sauce gloss and body, the lemon lifts everything without announcing itself.

14. **Serve.** Return the beef and shallots to the pot and warm through for 2 minutes. Scatter with flat-leaf parsley. Serve over chapli naan, steamed basmati, or buttered egg noodles.

## Variations

**Cheaper cut:** Beef neck or bone-in short ribs work well and often cost less than shin. Short ribs need 30–45 minutes of extra braising time. Add 100 ml stock if the liquid reduces too much.

**Without verjus:** Replace with 150 ml pomegranate juice for a more assertive, Middle Eastern-adjacent sourness, or 100 ml tamarind water plus 50 ml stock for something tangier and more Pakistani in character.

**Lamb pasanda version:** Replace beef with bone-in lamb shoulder cut into large pieces (about 800 g / 1.75 lb). Reduce braising time to 1 hour 30 minutes. The papaya marinade is equally effective on lamb.

**Mushroom addition:** If you want to echo the mushroom element of a classic bourguignon, sauté 200 g (7 oz) of quartered cremini mushrooms in butter separately and stir them into the pot in the final 15 minutes of cooking.

## Storage and Reheating

Transfer cooled leftovers to an airtight container within two hours of cooking. Refrigerate for up to 3 days. The sauce will solidify around the meat as it chills — this is normal and desirable; the gelatin from the collagen is doing its job.

To reheat: transfer to a saucepan, add 3–4 tablespoons of water or stock, cover, and warm over low heat for 12–15 minutes, turning the meat occasionally. Do not use a microwave; the collagen-rich sauce heats unevenly and the meat dries at its edges.

Freeze for up to 2 months in portions. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator before reheating.

## FAQ

### What cut of beef is best for beef bourguignon pasanda?
Beef shin is the best cut for this dish because its high collagen content dissolves into a silky sauce during the long braise. Chuck is a close second and slightly more widely available.

### Can I make beef bourguignon pasanda without papaya?
You can, but the beef will be less tender. Substitute with 3 tablespoons of kiwi fruit pulp and limit marinating time to 2 hours, or simply extend the braising time by 45 minutes.

### Is this beef pasanda recipe halal?
Yes. The recipe contains no pork, no alcohol, and no shellfish. Verjus is unfermented grape juice, not wine; it is halal. The beef should be sourced from a halal butcher as usual.

### Why verjus instead of red wine in a bourguignon pasanda?
Traditional beef bourguignon uses wine for acidity, body, and a faint sweetness. Verjus provides the acidity and fruitiness without fermentation. The result is lighter and less tannic, which actually suits the spice profile of a pasanda well.

### How long should I marinate the beef in papaya?
Between 4 and 6 hours. Under 4 hours the papain does not penetrate deeply enough. Over 6 hours it begins to degrade the muscle fibre surface, giving the meat an unpleasant mushy texture at the edges.

### Can I cook beef bourguignon pasanda in a pressure cooker?
Yes. After step 10, cook under high pressure for 35 minutes, then natural-release for 15 minutes. The texture will be tender but the sauce will be thinner; plan to reduce it for an extra 10–15 minutes after opening the cooker.

### What do I serve with this dish?
Steamed basmati rice is the most natural pairing given the spice profile. Buttered egg noodles work if you are leaning into the French structure. Warm naan is the most satisfying option if you want to eat the sauce without missing any of it.
