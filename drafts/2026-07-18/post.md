## Headnote

Morocco sits twenty kilometres from Spain and culturally straddles France in ways that most food writing underestimates. The tagine is not exotic to a French kitchen — it is simply a braise in a conical clay pot, the same logic as a daube or a pot-au-feu, but with a spice vocabulary that runs through ginger, saffron, and preserved lemon rather than thyme and bay. This beef and olive version is the kind of thing a Moroccan family in Lyon makes on a Sunday without drama. The potatoes absorb the braising liquid, the olives cut the fat, and the preserved lemon does the thing that citrus always does — it lifts the whole dish in the final minutes without making anything taste lemony. One pot. No stock required. The meat provides everything.

## Ingredients

### Spice and aromatics base
- 1 large onion, thinly sliced
- 3 garlic cloves, grated
- 1 tsp ground ginger
- 1 tsp ground turmeric
- ½ tsp ground black pepper
- 2 tsp salt (adjust at the end)
- 1 pinch saffron threads (about 20 threads), optional but recommended
- 1 green cayenne chilli, whole (optional)
- 80 ml (⅓ cup) extra-virgin olive oil

### Main
- 500 g (1.1 lb) lean halal beef, cut into 4 cm (1.5 in) cubes
- 700 g (1.5 lb) potatoes, peeled and cut into wedges
- 1 large handful green or red olives, pitted or unpitted
- ½ preserved lemon, quartered lengthwise, flesh scraped and discarded, rind only
- 700 ml (3 cups) water

### Finish
- 1 small handful flat-leaf parsley or coriander, roughly chopped

## Ingredient Notes

**Preserved lemon** — this is the one ingredient worth hunting down rather than substituting. Pakistani grocery stores in Paris stock it alongside Moroccan condiments; so do most épiceries orientales. In a pinch, combine 1 tsp lemon zest with ½ tsp flaky salt and add it with thirty seconds to go, but the depth is not the same.

**Saffron** — bloom it in 2 tbsp of warm water for ten minutes before using. This is not optional if you are using it; dry saffron thrown into a pot releases colour but not flavour.

**Beef cut** — the recipe calls for lean beef, which technically means less collagen, which means less body in the sauce. Chuck or blade shoulder, both cheap and widely available, will give you a richer result in the same cooking time. A true lean rump cube will cook faster but the sauce will be thinner.

**Olives** — green Castelvetrano olives or the smaller Picholine are the right shape and brininess here. If using olives packed in brine, rinse them once. If using salt-cured olives, rinse twice and taste before adding any additional salt to the pot.

**Cayenne chilli** — adding it whole and intact means heat stays mild; bruise it or split it if you want warmth throughout. Remove before serving if cooking for children.

## Method

1. **Bloom the saffron.** If using saffron, place the threads in a small bowl with 2 tbsp warm (not boiling) water. Set aside for ten minutes. This extracts the picrocrocin and safranal — the compounds responsible for flavour and colour — far more efficiently than adding the threads dry.

2. **Build the base.** In a wide, heavy-bottomed pot (or a tagine if you have one and a heat diffuser), combine the olive oil, sliced onion, grated garlic, ginger, turmeric, black pepper, and salt. Place over medium heat and cook, stirring occasionally, until the onion softens and turns pale gold — about eight minutes. You are not caramelising; you are sweating. The spices should smell toasted but not catch.

3. **Add the beef.** Nestle the beef cubes into the onion mixture and turn to coat. Cook, stirring occasionally, for five minutes so the outside of the meat seals in the spice paste. The beef will release some liquid; let it reduce before moving on.

4. **Add liquid and potatoes.** Pour in the 700 ml water and the bloomed saffron with its soaking water. Add the potato wedges and the whole cayenne chilli if using. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce to a steady simmer. Cover and cook for thirty minutes.

5. **Check the potatoes.** After thirty minutes, a knife should meet only a little resistance at the centre of the thickest wedge. If they are still hard, continue for another ten minutes. The potatoes should not disintegrate; they carry texture.

6. **Add the olives and preserved lemon rind.** Scatter the olives into the pot and lay the preserved lemon quarters over the top. Do not stir them in; let them sit on the surface and perfume the steam for the final ten minutes of covered cooking.

7. **Taste and adjust.** Uncover, taste the broth. The salt level should feel right; the preserved lemon will have added some, so adjust carefully. If the sauce is too thin for your preference, raise the heat and let it reduce uncovered for five minutes.

8. **Finish and serve.** Scatter the chopped parsley or coriander over the top. Bring the pot to the table. Serve with crusty bread or couscous to absorb the braising liquid.

## Variations

**Cheaper cut, deeper flavour.** Use 500 g bone-in beef short ribs or osso buco-style beef shin in place of diced lean beef. Increase the cooking time to 75-90 minutes. The collagen from the bone enriches the sauce into something that coats the back of a spoon.

**Lamb version.** Substitute the beef with 500 g bone-in lamb shoulder pieces. Reduce total cooking time by about ten minutes, as lamb shoulder at this size cooks faster. The saffron becomes more prominent against lamb; worth including.

**Vegetarian.** Omit the beef entirely. Double the potatoes to 1.4 kg and add one 400 g tin of chickpeas, drained, in step four. The chickpeas absorb the braising liquid well and give enough body. Reduce the water to 500 ml.

**Tomato version.** Add two medium tomatoes, grated on a box grater (skins discarded), in step two with the onions. This is a common Moroccan variant; the tomato adds sweetness and colour and makes the sauce a little thicker from the start.

## Storage and Reheating

Allow the tagine to cool to room temperature, then transfer to an airtight container. Refrigerate for up to three days. The flavour improves on day two as the saffron and preserved lemon continue to infuse the braising liquid.

Reheat in a covered pan over low heat with 2-3 tbsp of water added to prevent the potatoes from sticking. Do not microwave the potatoes; they turn grainy. If reheating from frozen (freeze for up to two months), thaw overnight in the refrigerator before reheating on the stovetop.

## FAQ

### Can I make this in a regular pot instead of a tagine?
Yes. A wide, heavy-bottomed sauté pan with a lid or a Dutch oven works identically. The tagine's conical lid returns condensation to the base, which is its one functional advantage. A tight-fitting lid on a heavy pot does the same job at lower cost.

### My sauce is very thin. What went wrong?
Most likely the beef released more water than expected, or the lid was not tight enough. Uncover the pot and raise the heat to medium-high for five to eight minutes to reduce. Lean beef gives less body than fattier cuts, so the sauce will always be lighter than a shoulder or rib braise.

### Do I need to sear the beef first?
This recipe does not call for searing, and that is deliberate. Browning the meat in a separate pan before adding it would add a layer of Maillard-reaction flavour, but it also adds fifteen minutes and another pan. The result here is brighter and more aromatic, not meatier. Both are valid; they are different dishes.

### The preserved lemon is very salty. How much do I use?
Half a lemon is the right amount for 500 g of meat. Scrape away the flesh and use only the rind; the flesh is where most of the salt and bitterness concentrate. Taste before you add any finishing salt.

### Can I add other vegetables?
Carrots work well — cut them slightly thicker than the potato wedges so they finish at the same time. Courgette can be added in the last fifteen minutes; any earlier and it will turn to mush. Avoid leafy vegetables, which go grey and add bitterness.

### Is this really halal French cooking or just a Moroccan recipe?
It is a Moroccan recipe cooked the way a French-Moroccan family in Lyon cooks it — which is to say with decent olive oil, patience, and no apology for the saffron bill. The French technique here is restraint: not over-spicing, letting the braising liquid do the work, finishing with fresh herb. The bones of the dish are North African.

### Can I use dried parsley instead of fresh?
Skip it entirely if you have no fresh parsley or coriander. Dried parsley adds nothing at this stage; it has no volatile oils left to contribute. A squeeze of fresh lemon juice at the table serves the same function — brightness — and is more honest.
