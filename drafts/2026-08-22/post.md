## Ratatouille Masala

Ratatouille is a dish that rewards patience and punishes impatience equally. Cook each vegetable separately, let them collapse into themselves, and the result is something considerably more than a vegetable stew. Add a South Asian temper at the start, cumin, mustard seeds and turmeric bloomed in olive oil, and the dish does not become an Indian curry. It remains fundamentally Provençal in structure and temperament. What changes is the bass note: warm, slightly bitter, with that faint mineral quality that turmeric gives to anything it touches. I have been making some version of this ratatouille masala for about fifteen years, ever since I noticed that the spicing logic of a tarka and the technique logic of a French soffritto are not so different. Both ask you to build flavour before the main vegetables arrive. Both reward low heat and attention.

## Ingredients

### Vegetables

- 1 medium aubergine (about 350 g / 12 oz), cut into 2 cm (¾ in) cubes
- 2 medium courgettes (about 400 g / 14 oz total), cut into 2 cm (¾ in) half-moons
- 1 red bell pepper (about 200 g / 7 oz), cut into 2 cm (¾ in) pieces
- 1 yellow bell pepper (about 200 g / 7 oz), cut into 2 cm (¾ in) pieces
- 400 g (14 oz) ripe tomatoes, roughly chopped, or one 400 g tin whole plum tomatoes, crushed by hand
- 1 medium onion (about 180 g / 6 oz), finely sliced
- 4 garlic cloves, thinly sliced

### Temper and spice

- 5 tbsp (75 ml) olive oil, divided
- 1 tsp cumin seeds
- 1 tsp brown mustard seeds
- ½ tsp ground turmeric
- 1 tsp ground coriander
- ½ tsp Kashmiri red chilli powder (or mild paprika for less heat)
- 1 tsp fine sea salt, plus more to taste
- ½ tsp black pepper, freshly ground

### Finishing

- 1 tsp red wine vinegar (or white wine vinegar)
- Small handful of fresh coriander leaves, roughly torn
- Small handful of fresh basil leaves, torn at the last moment

## Ingredient notes

**Brown mustard seeds:** Black mustard seeds also work and are more widely available in Pakistani grocers. The point is the pop and the slight bitterness they release into the oil. Yellow mustard seeds are milder and less interesting here.

**Kashmiri chilli powder:** This is not cayenne. It is mild, deeply red, and almost sweet. It gives colour without aggression. Find it at any South Asian grocery. If you cannot, use half sweet paprika and half a pinch of cayenne.

**Tomatoes:** Fresh ripe tomatoes in summer, tinned in winter. If using tinned, San Marzano or any good-quality whole plum tomato. Do not use tinned chopped tomatoes; they are waterier and sweeter.

**Olive oil:** Provençal logic here. This is not a dish for mustard oil or vegetable oil. Olive oil is structural, not just a cooking medium.

## Method

1. Salt the aubergine. Toss the aubergine cubes with a generous pinch of salt and leave them in a colander for 15 minutes. This draws out moisture and reduces the oil they absorb during frying. Pat dry with kitchen paper before cooking.

2. Cook the aubergine. Heat 2 tbsp olive oil in a wide, heavy pan or Dutch oven over medium-high heat. Add the aubergine in a single layer (do this in two batches if needed) and fry until each piece is golden brown on at least two sides, 6 to 8 minutes. Remove to a plate. The aubergine should be nearly cooked through; it will finish later.

3. Cook the courgettes. Add 1 tbsp oil to the same pan. Add the courgettes and cook over medium-high heat without stirring for the first 2 minutes, so they colour rather than steam. Cook until lightly golden, about 5 minutes total. Remove to the plate with the aubergine.

4. Cook the peppers. Add a splash more oil if needed. Add the red and yellow peppers and cook over medium heat until they soften and take on a little colour at the edges, about 6 minutes. Remove to the plate.

5. Build the temper. Reduce heat to medium-low. Add the remaining olive oil to the pan. Once the oil shimmers, add the cumin seeds and mustard seeds. They will pop and crackle within 30 to 45 seconds. At that point, add the sliced onion. This sequence matters: the seeds need direct hot oil to bloom properly, not steam from the onions.

6. Cook the onion. Cook the onion over medium-low heat, stirring occasionally, until it is deep golden and beginning to caramelise, about 12 minutes. Do not rush this. Add the garlic and cook for a further 2 minutes until fragrant.

7. Add the ground spices. Add the turmeric, ground coriander, and Kashmiri chilli powder. Stir for 30 seconds over low heat. Ground spices burn in seconds if the heat is too high or the pan too dry; if necessary, add a small splash of water to prevent scorching.

8. Add the tomatoes. Add the chopped fresh tomatoes or crushed tinned tomatoes. Increase heat to medium and cook, stirring occasionally, until the tomatoes collapse and the oil begins to separate at the edges of the pan, about 10 minutes. A masala is ready when the oil rises.

9. Combine everything. Return the cooked aubergine, courgettes and peppers to the pan. Stir gently to coat everything in the tomato masala. Season with salt and black pepper. Reduce heat to low, cover partially, and cook for 10 minutes, allowing the flavours to integrate. The vegetables should be tender but not dissolving.

10. Finish and serve. Add the red wine vinegar, stir once, and taste for seasoning. The vinegar lifts the whole dish; do not skip it. Scatter fresh coriander and torn basil over the top just before serving.

## Variations

**Add chickpeas:** Stir in 240 g (1 cup) cooked or tinned chickpeas at step 9. This makes the dish more substantial and adds protein. It is no longer ratatouille but it is very good.

**Fennel instead of one pepper:** Replace one bell pepper with half a fennel bulb, thinly sliced, cooked alongside the onion. Fennel's anise note works well with both the Provençal tomato base and the cumin.

**Increase the heat:** Add one finely chopped green chilli with the garlic in step 6. This moves the dish closer to a proper sabzi; it is a matter of preference.

**Different summer squash:** Patty pan or yellow squash work exactly as courgettes do. Cut them to a similar 2 cm size so cooking time stays consistent.

## Storage and reheating

Cool completely and refrigerate in an airtight container for up to 4 days. The flavour genuinely improves on day two, as the spices have time to settle into the vegetables. Reheat gently in a pan over medium-low heat with a small splash of water; the olive oil will re-emulsify as the dish warms. The dish freezes well for up to 2 months. Freeze in portions. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator and reheat as above. Do not add the fresh herbs until after reheating.

## FAQ

### What makes this a ratatouille masala rather than just a spiced ratatouille?

Ratatouille masala uses a South Asian temper, whole cumin and mustard seeds bloomed in olive oil before the base is built, which is a structural technique difference from standard ratatouille. Most spiced ratatouille recipes simply stir ground spices into an existing Provençal base; this recipe builds the masala from the oil up, which produces a deeper, more integrated flavour.

### Do I really need to cook each vegetable separately?

Yes. Aubergine, courgette and peppers have different moisture contents and cook at different rates. Cooking them together steams the lot into a uniform soft mass. Cooking them separately lets each develop its own caramelised edges, which gives the finished dish textural and flavour contrast.

### Can I make this in the oven, confit-style?

You can roast each vegetable on a separate tray at 200°C (400°F) until golden, about 20 to 25 minutes, then combine them with the stovetop tomato masala. The result is slightly drier and more concentrated; some people prefer it. The core technique for the masala base stays the same.

### Is this dish suitable for meal prep?

This ratatouille masala is an excellent meal-prep dish. It tastes better the next day, holds in the refrigerator for four days, and freezes without quality loss. Make a double batch; it scales without difficulty.

### What do I serve this with?

Over plain basmati rice, with warm naan, or alongside crusty sourdough bread that can pick up the olive-oil-rich tomato sauce. It works as a side dish to grilled lamb chops or as a main in its own right with bread and a simple raita.

### My masala is bitter. What went wrong?

Bitter masala almost always means the ground spices or mustard seeds burned. Mustard seeds are ready when they pop; another 30 seconds and they turn acrid. Ground spices need no more than 30 seconds over medium heat before liquid arrives. A pinch of sugar and an extra splash of tomato can rescue a mildly bitter batch; badly burned spices require starting the base again.

### Can I use dried herbs instead of fresh basil and coriander?

For the coriander, dried is a poor substitute at the finishing stage; it lacks the brightness the fresh leaf provides. For basil, dried basil loses its floral quality entirely when added cold to a finished dish. If you cannot get fresh herbs, finish with a squeeze of lemon juice instead; the acidity does some of the same lifting work.
