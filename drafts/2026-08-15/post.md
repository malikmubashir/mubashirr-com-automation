## Headnote

Guinea fowl is what happens when a chicken decides it has more going on. The flesh is darker, leaner, and carries a faint gaminess that calls for a sauce with backbone. Korma — properly made, not the cream-flooded hotel version — has exactly that backbone: whole spices bloomed in fat, onions cooked past softness into something almost jammy, and yoghurt added in small additions so it never breaks. The marriage here is not cosmetic. The pintade's slight wildness holds up against the cardamom and toasted almond paste where a regular chicken would disappear into it. I make this in a Dutch oven, start to finish, and the result sits somewhere between a Pakistani dastarkhwan and a French table d'hôte — comfortably at home in neither, which is exactly the point.

## Ingredients

### The Bird

- 1 guinea fowl (pintade), about 1.4 kg (3 lb), jointed into 8 pieces, skin on
- 1 tsp fine sea salt
- ½ tsp ground turmeric

### Almond Paste

- 70 g (½ cup) blanched almonds, lightly toasted
- 3 tbsp whole milk
- ½ tsp ground green cardamom (from about 8 pods)

### Korma Base

- 4 tbsp ghee or neutral oil
- 5 green cardamom pods, lightly crushed
- 3 cloves
- 1 black cardamom pod, cracked
- 2 bay leaves
- 1 small piece mace (about ¼ tsp ground if unavailable)
- 2 medium onions (about 320 g / 11 oz), thinly sliced
- 1 tbsp ginger paste (freshly grated, not jarred)
- 1 tbsp garlic paste (freshly grated)
- 1 tsp ground coriander
- ½ tsp ground cumin
- ¼ tsp white pepper
- 240 g (1 cup) full-fat plain yoghurt, at room temperature, whisked smooth
- 200 ml (¾ cup + 1 tbsp) water, plus more as needed
- 1 tsp kewra water (screwpine essence), added at the end
- Fine sea salt to taste

### To Finish

- 1 tbsp single cream (optional, for gloss)
- A pinch of saffron steeped in 2 tbsp warm milk (optional)
- 1 tbsp toasted flaked almonds
- 4–5 dried rose petals, unsprayed

## Ingredient Notes

**Guinea fowl (pintade):** Available from French butchers (any decent volaillier will have it) and increasingly from UK halal butchers. Order halal-certified bird specifically — ask; do not assume. If you buy whole, joint it yourself or ask the butcher. Keep the back and neck for stock another day.

**Black cardamom:** Smoky, eucalyptus-forward, and irreplaceable here. The green pods provide floral sweetness; black cardamom provides the low note. Find it at any South Asian grocery. Do not substitute green.

**Kewra water:** Distilled from screwpine flowers. Adds a specific floral-musky quality to the finish that rosewater does not replicate. A few drops is enough; more makes it soapy. South Asian grocery stores stock it cheaply.

**Almond paste:** Toast the almonds lightly (5 minutes in a dry pan until they smell warm) before blending. Raw almonds make the paste taste flat.

**Yoghurt:** Must be room temperature and whisked before it goes into the pan. Cold yoghurt dropped into hot fat will curdle. This is the one rule you cannot ignore.

## Method

1. **Salt and turmeric the bird.** Pat the guinea fowl pieces dry with paper towels. Rub evenly with 1 tsp salt and ½ tsp turmeric. Set aside at room temperature for 20 minutes while you prepare everything else. This brief cure seasons the meat through the skin, not just the surface.

2. **Make the almond paste.** Combine the toasted almonds, milk, and ground cardamom in a small blender. Process to a smooth, thick paste, scraping down the sides as needed. It should look like a rough marzipan. Set aside.

3. **Brown the guinea fowl.** Heat 2 tbsp ghee in a wide, heavy Dutch oven (5-litre / 5-quart) over medium-high heat. Brown the guinea fowl pieces in two batches, skin side down first, 4 minutes per side, until deep golden. The skin should release cleanly from the pan. Remove and set aside.

4. **Bloom the whole spices.** Reduce heat to medium. Add the remaining 2 tbsp ghee to the same pot. Add the green cardamom, cloves, black cardamom, bay leaves, and mace. Stir for 60 to 90 seconds. You are listening for a shift in the sizzle — it becomes quieter and the aroma opens up. This is correct.

5. **Cook the onions to deep mahogany.** Add the sliced onions and a small pinch of salt (it draws moisture and speeds caramelisation). Cook over medium heat, stirring every 4 to 5 minutes, for 30 to 35 minutes. You want them deep mahogany, not amber. Amber onions give you a pale, sweet korma; mahogany gives you depth and colour. Add a tablespoon of water if they stick in the last minutes.

6. **Add ginger and garlic.** Push the onions to the sides. Add the ginger and garlic pastes to the centre of the pot. Cook, stirring into the onions, for 3 to 4 minutes until the raw smell is gone and the paste has darkened slightly.

7. **Add the ground spices.** Add the ground coriander, cumin, and white pepper. Stir for 1 minute. This brief dry-frying of the ground spices in the fat concentrates their flavour before any liquid is introduced.

8. **Add the almond paste.** Stir in the almond-cardamom paste. Cook for 2 minutes, folding it into the onion masala until fully incorporated.

9. **Temper in the yoghurt (bhunai).** This is the critical step. Reduce heat to low. Add 2 tablespoons of the whisked yoghurt and stir vigorously into the masala until it is absorbed and the mixture has dried slightly. Repeat with the remaining yoghurt, two tablespoons at a time. This process — bhunai — takes about 10 minutes in total. You are cooking out the water from the yoghurt incrementally so the fat separates back out (you will see oily patches appear). That fat separation signals the korma base is ready.

10. **Return the guinea fowl.** Nestle the browned pieces into the masala, turning to coat. Add 200 ml water. Bring to a gentle simmer, cover with a tight-fitting lid, reduce heat to low, and braise for 45 to 55 minutes. Guinea fowl legs take longer than the breasts; if you are worried, remove the breasts after 35 minutes and let them rest while the legs finish.

11. **Check and finish.** The meat should be cooked through and pulling away from the bone. Taste and adjust salt. Add the kewra water, and the saffron milk if using, stirring gently. If using single cream, stir it in now for a glossier sauce. The sauce should coat a spoon thickly; if too thin, remove the lid and simmer uncovered for 5 minutes.

12. **Rest before serving.** Turn off the heat and let the pot sit, lid on, for 10 minutes. This rest lets the meat reabsorb some sauce. Scatter toasted flaked almonds and rose petals over the top just before bringing the pot to the table.

## Variations

**Bone-in chicken thighs instead of guinea fowl:** A halal guinea fowl recipe adapted this way loses the gaminess but keeps the technique intact. Use 1.2 kg (about 8 thighs), reduce braise time to 30 to 35 minutes.

**Pistachio paste in place of almond:** Replace almonds with 70 g unsalted, shelled pistachios. The sauce goes green-tinged and earthier. The flavour is less traditional but interesting.

**Vegetarian version:** Replace the bird with 600 g paneer cut into large cubes (shallow-fry before adding) and 2 medium waxy potatoes cut into quarters, par-boiled. Skip the initial browning step. Reduce braise time to 20 minutes.

**Richer, slightly spicier sauce:** Add ½ tsp Kashmiri chilli powder with the other ground spices and 1 tbsp cashew paste alongside the almond paste. The sauce becomes redder and more complex.

## Storage and Reheating

Cool completely before storing. Transfer to an airtight container and refrigerate for up to 3 days. The flavour improves on day two as the whole spices continue to infuse.

Reheat gently in a covered pan over low heat with 2 to 3 tablespoons of water to loosen the sauce. Do not boil; the yoghurt sauce can split if heated aggressively. For portions, a covered microwave at 60% power works well.

Freeze for up to 2 months. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator. Reheat as above, adding a little water if the sauce has thickened. Add fresh kewra water after reheating — the volatile aroma dissipates in the freezer.

## FAQ

### Can I use a whole guinea fowl without jointing it?

Guinea fowl korma works best with jointed pieces because the korma sauce cannot penetrate a whole bird evenly, and the breast will overcook before the legs are done. Ask your butcher to joint it if you prefer not to do it yourself.

### Why does my yoghurt curdle when I add it to the masala?

Yoghurt curdles in korma when it is cold or added too quickly to hot fat. Bring the yoghurt to room temperature, whisk it until smooth, and add it two tablespoons at a time over low heat, stirring continuously between additions.

### What does guinea fowl taste like compared to chicken?

Guinea fowl has darker, leaner meat than chicken with a mild gaminess similar to pheasant but less pronounced. It holds up well in a spiced braise where a chicken breast would become soft and flavourless.

### Is this the same as a white korma (shahi korma)?

This guinea fowl korma is related to shahi korma in its use of almond paste and whole spices, but the onions are cooked to a deep mahogany colour rather than kept light, so the sauce is darker and has more savoury depth. It is not a white korma.

### Can I make this without kewra water?

You can omit the kewra water; the dish still works. A few drops of rosewater can replace it for a different but not identical floral note. Kewra is worth buying — a small bottle costs almost nothing and keeps for a year.

### How do I know when the bhunai (yoghurt tempering) step is done?

Bhunai is complete when the ghee or oil visibly separates back out of the masala and pools around the edges of the mixture. The paste will look drier and denser than when you started. This typically takes 10 to 12 minutes if done over low heat.

### Can I cook this in advance for a dinner party?

Guinea fowl korma reheats very well and is arguably better the next day. Cook it fully, cool it, refrigerate overnight, and reheat gently before serving. Add a splash of water and the kewra water fresh when reheating.
