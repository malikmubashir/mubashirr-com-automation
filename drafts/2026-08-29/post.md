## Headnote

Nihari is a patience dish. You make it the night before, or you make it for hours on a cold Friday morning, and the whole thing collapses into something so deep and unctuous that bread feels insufficient. Steak au poivre is the opposite: fifteen minutes from cold pan to plate, all heat and nerve. The jus in this recipe takes the slow logic of nihari and compresses it into a sauce form — fennel seed, long pepper, mace, a darkened onion, marrow bones — then reduces it to the kind of liquid that coats a spoon and means something. The steak gets a French treatment: dry-brined, crusted in coarsely cracked black and long pepper, seared in a very hot pan. The result is not nihari, and it is not steak au poivre. It is a third thing, coherent on its own terms.

## Ingredients

### For the nihari jus
- 500 g (1.1 lb) beef marrow bones, cut into 5 cm (2-inch) rounds
- 300 g (10.5 oz) beef shin or shank, bone-in
- 1 large onion (about 200 g / 7 oz), halved, unpeeled
- 1.2 litres (5 cups) cold water
- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 1 tsp whole black peppercorns
- 1 tsp long pepper (pipli), roughly broken
- 1 tsp fennel seeds
- ½ tsp whole cumin seeds
- 4 green cardamom pods, lightly crushed
- 1 black cardamom pod
- 1 blade of mace
- 1 small piece of cassia bark (3 cm / 1.2 inches)
- ¼ tsp hing (a small pinch)
- 1 tbsp ginger-garlic paste
- 1 tsp fine sea salt, plus more to taste
- 2 tsp nihari masala (store-bought, such as National or Shan, or see note)
- 1 tbsp whole wheat flour (atta), dissolved in 3 tbsp cold water
- 15 g (½ oz) cold unsalted butter (or clarified butter for a richer finish)

### For the steak
- 2 beef sirloin or ribeye steaks, each about 250 g (9 oz), at least 2.5 cm (1 inch) thick — halal-certified
- 1½ tsp coarsely cracked black peppercorns
- ½ tsp coarsely cracked long pepper (pipli)
- 1 tsp flaky sea salt
- 1 tbsp neutral oil (sunflower or grapeseed)
- 1 tbsp unsalted butter
- 2 garlic cloves, skin on, lightly crushed
- 2 sprigs fresh thyme

## Ingredient notes

**Long pepper (pipli):** Not the same heat profile as black pepper. It is warmer, slightly sweet, with a numbing quality at the back of the throat. Nihari masalas often include it already; adding whole cracked pipli to the steak crust brings that flavour forward. South Asian grocery stores stock it dried.

**Hing:** A tiny quantity is important here. It rounds out the fermented depth that nihari normally develops over hours. Do not skip it or double it.

**Nihari masala:** Use a pre-made blend as a base. National and Shan are reliably consistent. If you are making your own, the core spices are dry ginger powder, fennel, anise, coriander, and a small amount of dried rose petal.

**Marrow bones:** Ask your halal butcher to cut them into rounds rather than lengthwise. The marrow will release into the jus and give it body without flour doing all the work.

**No alcohol:** Traditional steak au poivre uses cognac for the flambé. This recipe does not. What the cognac does is deglaze caramelised fond and add a fruity acidity. Here, the nihari jus carries that complexity on its own. This is not a cognac-free steak au poivre with something missing; it is a different equation that lands in a different place.

## Method

**Making the nihari jus (can be done the day before)**

1. Place the marrow bones and beef shin in a cold pot. Cover with cold water. Bring to a boil, then immediately drain and rinse the bones under cold water. This removes impurities and gives a cleaner jus.

2. In a heavy-based saucepan or Dutch oven, char the onion halves cut-side down directly over a gas flame, or under a hot grill, until deep black on the cut surface. This gives the jus its characteristic brown colour and a slight bitterness that balances the spices.

3. Heat 1 tbsp oil in the same pot over medium heat. Add the whole peppercorns, pipli, fennel, cumin, both cardamoms, mace, and cassia. Stir for 45 seconds until fragrant — you are toasting, not burning.

4. Add the hing. Stir for five seconds. Add the ginger-garlic paste. Cook, stirring, for two minutes until the raw smell disappears.

5. Add the blanched bones and shin, the charred onion halves, 1 tsp salt, and the nihari masala. Pour over 1.2 litres cold water. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce to a low simmer, partially covered, for 2 hours.

6. Strain the stock through a fine sieve, pressing the solids. You should have roughly 500–600 ml (2–2.5 cups). Discard the spent solids. Return the liquid to a clean saucepan.

7. Whisk the atta-water slurry into the strained jus. Bring to a simmer over medium heat, whisking constantly for two minutes. The flour thickens slightly — this is the classical nihari texture, a gentle velvet rather than a sharp reduction. Continue to simmer uncovered until the jus reduces to about 250 ml (1 cup) and coats a spoon. Taste and adjust salt. Set aside. You can refrigerate this for up to two days.

**Cooking the steak**

8. Pat the steaks completely dry with paper towels. Salt them generously with flaky sea salt on all sides. Rest on a rack, uncovered, in the refrigerator for at least one hour (or overnight). This dry brine firms the exterior so it sears rather than steams.

9. Mix the cracked black pepper and cracked pipli together. Press the mixture firmly onto both flat faces of each steak. The crust should be visible and thick, not a dusting.

10. Take the steaks out of the refrigerator 20 minutes before cooking. Heat a heavy cast-iron skillet until it is smoking — not warm, not hot, smoking. Add the oil.

11. Lay the steaks away from you into the pan. Do not move them. Sear for 2.5 to 3 minutes per side for medium-rare (internal temperature 52–54°C / 125–130°F before resting). For a 2.5 cm steak this is a reliable guide; adjust by 30 seconds per additional 5 mm of thickness.

12. In the final 30 seconds, add the butter, crushed garlic, and thyme to the pan. Tilt the pan and baste the steaks continuously with the foaming butter.

13. Transfer the steaks to a warm plate. Rest for five minutes. The internal temperature will rise to 57–59°C (135–138°F), which is a proper medium-rare.

**Finishing and plating**

14. While the steak rests, warm the nihari jus over medium-low heat. Whisk in the cold butter in small pieces to give it a slight gloss and body. Do not boil after adding the butter or it will split.

15. Spoon 3–4 tablespoons of jus into each warmed plate, spreading slightly. Slice the steak if you prefer, or plate it whole. Lay the steak on or beside the jus. A final pinch of flaky salt on the cut face of the meat.

## Variations

**Cheaper cut:** Replace sirloin with beef flank steak. Slice it against the grain after resting and serve the strips fanned over the jus. The texture is different — chewier, more mineral — but it suits the nihari jus well.

**More heat:** Add two dried red Kashmiri chillies to the jus during the simmering stage. They add colour and a medium, rounded heat without sharpening the flavour the way green chillies would.

**Without marrow bones:** Use a good-quality halal beef stock (not bouillon cubes) and reduce it with the same spice toasting and hing step from the beginning of the method. The result is thinner and less unctuous, but still worthwhile.

**Vegetarian jus version:** Toast the same spice profile in oil, add caramelised onion and roasted beetroot (which provides colour and earthiness), simmer in mushroom stock, and thicken with atta. The steak becomes a portobello mushroom slab, pan-seared in the same manner.

## Storage and reheating

The nihari jus keeps refrigerated in an airtight container for three days. It also freezes cleanly for up to two months — portion it in 125 ml quantities so you can defrost what you need. Reheat gently over low heat, whisking to recombine any separated fat.

Cooked steaks do not store well and should be eaten immediately. If you have leftover steak, slice it cold the next day and serve over warmed jus with flatbread — it functions well as a next-day open sandwich.

## FAQ

### What is steak au poivre nihari?
Steak au poivre nihari is a halal fusion dish that combines a French pepper-crusted beef steak with a reduced, spiced jus based on the flavour profile of Pakistani nihari, using marrow bones, long pepper, mace, and hing to build depth.

### Can I make the nihari jus ahead of time?
Yes. The nihari jus is better made one or two days in advance and refrigerated; the spice flavours settle and integrate overnight in a way they do not when the jus is used immediately.

### What cut of beef works best for this recipe?
Sirloin or ribeye both work well. They are well-marbled enough to sear without drying, and thick enough to develop a proper crust while staying medium-rare inside. Fillet is leaner and cooks faster — reduce searing time by 30 seconds per side.

### Is there a substitute for long pepper (pipli)?
If you cannot find pipli, use a ratio of three parts black pepper to one part ground white pepper as a rough substitute for the crust. The warmth is not identical, but the layered pepper quality is preserved.

### Why is hing used in such a small amount?
Hing is potent and the goal here is not to taste it as a distinct note but to use it as a background amplifier that deepens the savoury quality of the jus. A quantity larger than a small pinch will dominate.

### How do I know the steak is at the right doneness without a thermometer?
Press the centre of the steak: it should yield like the base of your thumb when your hand is relaxed, with slight resistance. This is medium-rare. Firmer means more cooked; very soft means underdone. That said, a probe thermometer is a worthwhile ten-euro investment for steak cookery.

### Can I use a non-stick pan instead of cast iron?
A non-stick pan will not develop the same crust because it cannot sustain the temperatures required. Use stainless steel as an alternative to cast iron. The pan must be genuinely hot before the steak goes in.
