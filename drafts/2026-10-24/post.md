## Headnote

Haleem is already a masterclass in patience. You cook meat and grains together until they surrender into something that is neither soup nor stew but its own category entirely. The version I make now layers that logic with a French braise: the lamb shanks go into the pot whole, are seared until properly brown, and braised low and slow before the meat is pulled and folded into the grain porridge. This matters. The shank's collagen dissolves into the cooking liquid, giving you gelatin that haleem made with boneless meat never quite achieves. The result is richer, more unctuous, and holds together on the spoon in that specific way that good haleem should. Pearl barley does the work of the traditional wheat here — it swells, softens, and thickens more reliably than whole wheat in a home kitchen. This is a lamb shank haleem built for a Saturday, not a shortcut.

## Ingredients

### The braise
- 2 lamb shanks (approx. 1.2 kg / 2 lb 10 oz total)
- 2 tbsp neutral oil
- 1 large onion, halved and thinly sliced
- 6 garlic cloves, crushed
- 25 g (1 oz) fresh ginger, grated
- 1 tsp cumin seeds
- 1 tsp coriander seeds, lightly crushed
- 1 tsp turmeric
- 1½ tsp Kashmiri red chilli powder
- 1½ tsp salt
- 1.2 litres (5 cups) water or light lamb stock

### The grain and lentil base
- 120 g (⅔ cup) pearl barley, soaked 1 hour, drained
- 80 g (⅓ cup) chana dal (split chickpea lentils), soaked 30 min, drained
- 60 g (¼ cup) masoor dal (red split lentils), no soaking needed
- 1 tsp salt

### Finishing
- 1½ tsp garam masala (see ingredient notes)
- 1 tsp dried fenugreek leaves (kasuri methi), crushed between palms
- 1 tbsp ghee or good butter
- Juice of ½ lemon

### Tarka (tempering oil)
- 3 tbsp neutral oil
- 1 medium onion, sliced thin
- 4 dried red chillies
- 1 tsp cumin seeds

### To serve
- Fresh ginger, julienned
- Green chillies, sliced
- Fresh coriander, chopped
- Lemon wedges

## Ingredient notes

**Lamb shanks:** Front shanks work as well as hind here. What you need is the bone and its marrow — they give the braising liquid body that nothing else replaces. Ask your halal butcher to leave the shank whole.

**Garam masala:** Do not use a pre-ground mix older than three months. If you have the components, grind equal parts black cardamom (seeds only), green cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, and black pepper. Two minutes with a spice grinder, and you have something significantly better than a jar from two years ago.

**Chana dal:** This is split Bengal gram, not yellow split peas. The texture is different: firmer, earthier, slower to collapse. South Asian grocery stores or online. It gives the haleem its faint nuttiness.

**Kashmiri chilli powder:** Mild heat, vivid red colour, fruity in the background. If unavailable, use half sweet paprika and half regular chilli powder.

**Pearl barley:** Standard supermarket barley works. Do not use quick-cook barley — it disintegrates too early and the texture ends up gluey rather than thick.

## Method

1. **Sear the shanks.** Pat the lamb shanks dry. Heat oil in a wide, heavy-bottomed pot (a Dutch oven or cocotte is ideal) over high heat until shimmering. Sear the shanks on all sides until deeply browned — 8 to 10 minutes total. Do not rush this. The colour here is not cosmetic; it is flavour. Remove and set aside.

2. **Build the masala.** Reduce heat to medium. Add the sliced onion to the same pot and cook, stirring occasionally, until deep mahogany — not amber, not golden. This takes 18 to 22 minutes over medium heat. Add garlic and ginger, stir for 90 seconds until raw smell leaves. Add cumin seeds, crushed coriander seeds, turmeric, Kashmiri chilli, and salt. Stir for one minute until the spices are fragrant and the paste is beginning to stick.

3. **Braise the shanks.** Return the shanks to the pot. Pour in the water or stock. Bring to a boil, skim any foam, then reduce to a gentle simmer. Cover tightly and cook for 2 hours, turning the shanks once at the halfway point, until the meat is falling away from the bone.

4. **Cook the grains and lentils separately.** While the shanks braise, put the drained barley, chana dal, and masoor dal in a separate pot with 900 ml (3¾ cups) water and 1 tsp salt. Bring to a boil, reduce to a simmer, and cook covered for 45 to 55 minutes, until all three are completely soft and the liquid is mostly absorbed. The masoor will dissolve; the chana dal and barley will retain some texture. This is correct.

5. **Pull the meat.** Lift the cooked shanks out. Remove the bones (they should slip out cleanly). Pull the meat into rough shreds using two forks or your fingers. Reserve the braising liquid.

6. **Combine.** Add the cooked grain mixture to the braising pot along with the pulled meat. Stir well to combine. At this point the haleem should be thick and cohesive — it moves like loose porridge. If it is too thin, cook uncovered over medium heat, stirring frequently, for 10 to 15 minutes. If too thick, add braising liquid or hot water in 50 ml increments.

7. **Finish the seasoning.** Stir in the garam masala, kasuri methi, ghee, and lemon juice. Taste and adjust salt. Cook for a further 5 minutes on low heat, stirring constantly to prevent catching on the bottom.

8. **Make the tarka.** In a small frying pan, heat oil over medium-high until hot. Add sliced onion and cook until it is crisp and deep brown at the edges — around 10 to 12 minutes. Add dried chillies and cumin seeds, stir for 30 seconds, then pour the entire tarka over the haleem in the pot. Do not stir it in before serving; let the table see it.

9. **Serve.** Ladle into bowls. Top with julienned ginger, green chillies, and fresh coriander. Lemon wedges on the side.

## Variations

**Slower, richer (tagine haleem approach):** After step 3, transfer everything to a clay tagine or a covered baking dish and finish in the oven at 150°C (300°F) for the full braise time. The indirect heat gives more even collagen extraction and a slightly sweeter, more rounded broth. The grain step stays on the stovetop.

**Lamb shank barley stew version (lighter):** Skip the chana dal and masoor. Double the barley to 220 g (1¼ cups). Do not blend or pound anything — keep the texture chunky. This is closer to a French-style barley broth with Pakistani spice and less haleem, more harira-adjacent. Finish with a tablespoon of thick yogurt stirred in off the heat.

**Bone marrow addition:** Ask the butcher to include two split marrow bones. Roast them at 220°C (425°F) for 15 minutes while you make the tarka. Scoop the marrow into the haleem at the finish. It folds in and adds a layer of richness that is difficult to explain without tasting.

**Vegetarian base:** Replace the shanks with 400 g (14 oz) canned brown lentils (drained) and use a good vegetable stock. Add 2 tbsp of good olive oil during the masala stage. Without the collagen, the texture is thinner; stir in 1 tbsp of cornflour dissolved in cold water if you want it to thicken. The spice logic stays the same.

## Storage and reheating

Haleem thickens considerably in the fridge. Store in an airtight container for up to 4 days. Reheat in a saucepan over medium-low heat with 3 to 4 tbsp of water stirred in to loosen it; haleem scorches easily on high heat. Stir frequently.

Freezes well for up to 2 months. Portion before freezing. Thaw overnight in the fridge, then reheat as above. Make a fresh tarka when serving from frozen — the old one goes limp in storage.

## FAQ

### Can I make lamb shank haleem in a pressure cooker?
Yes. Sear and make the masala in the pot as written, then pressure cook the shanks with the braising liquid for 40 minutes at high pressure. Cook the grains separately on the stovetop as directed; pressure cooking them together with the meat tends to over-soften the barley before the chana dal is done.

### What is the difference between haleem and harissa?
Haleem is a South Asian dish of slow-cooked meat pounded or stirred with grains and lentils, seasoned with garam masala and chilli. Harissa (the Levantine dish, not the paste) is its Turkish and Arab cousin, traditionally made with wheat and chicken. The spicing, grain ratios, and finishing technique differ significantly.

### Why do I cook the grains separately from the meat?
Cooking them together risks either under-braised meat or over-cooked, gluey grains. Separate cooking gives you control over each element. The grains finish in about an hour; the shanks need two. Combining at the end lets both hit their optimal texture before they meet.

### Can I use mutton instead of lamb shanks?
Mutton shanks work and the flavour is more intense and gamier, which many people prefer in haleem. Increase the braise time by 30 to 45 minutes and check tenderness before combining with the grains.

### My haleem is too thin. How do I fix it?
Remove the lid and cook over medium heat, stirring frequently, until it reduces to the consistency you want. Alternatively, take a ladleful of the grain mixture, blend it smooth, and stir it back in — this is a common restaurant technique and works quickly.

### Is this a halal haleem recipe?
This recipe contains no pork, no alcohol, and no non-halal gelatin. The lamb should be sourced from a halal-certified butcher, which is the only variable in your control.

### What bread goes with this?
Khamiri roti is traditional in Lahori haleem service. If you cannot make it, a good naan works. In France, I have served this with a lightly toasted sourdough demi-baguette when the kids are eating, and it holds up well — the bread is not traditional but the haleem does not care.
